# Wataoka et al. 2024 — Self-preference bias in LLM-as-a-Judge

- **Full reference and link:** Wataoka, K.; Takahashi, T.; Ri, R. (SB Intuitions). *Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge*. arXiv:2410.21819 (v2, 2025-06-21; first version Oct/2024). <https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819> · Publication venue: **not stated** in the text read (only the arXiv stamp).

- **Convention:** **[Paper]** = stated/measured; **[Interpretation]** = my reading.

## 1. Problem the paper addresses

LLMs used as judges tend to **overrate their own outputs** (self-preference bias), which can embed the judge's style/ideology into the systems being evaluated (§1). What was missing was (a) a **quantitative metric** of the bias comparable to humans (previous works, such as Panickssery et al., relate self-recognition but "lacked a comparison with human evaluators"; Xu et al. and Stureborg et al. use an absolute score, which is hard to anchor) and (b) an **explanation of the cause**. The authors choose **pairwise** evaluation because it allows more consistent human labels (§1).

## 2. Method (step by step)

**2.1 Metric (§3–4.1)**
- Basis: **Equal Opportunity** (Hardt et al. 2016, Eq. 1): a classifier is fair if it has **the same recall** across groups with different sensitive attributes: P(Y'=1|S=1,Y=1) = P(Y'=1|S=0,Y=1) (measures whether the classifier is equally accurate in both groups).
- **Definition 4.1 / Eq. 2 (self-preference bias of evaluator f):** `Bias = P(Y'=1 | S=1, Y=1) − P(Y'=1 | S=0, Y=1)`, where, for a pair of responses (y0, y1): Y = index of the response preferred by the **human**; Y' = index of the one preferred by **judge f**; S = index of the response **generated by f itself**. That is: (**how much the judge agrees with the human when the judge's own response is the one the human prefers**) − (**how much it agrees when it is the other model's response that the human prefers**). 0 = no bias; close to 1 = high bias; −1 = reverse bias (underrates its own responses).
- **Ambiguity I record [Interpretation]:** the literal Eq. 2 conditions both terms on Y=1, but the prose of §4.1 and the calculation of §4.3 treat the 2nd term as "the conditional probability of the evaluator f rating itself unfavorably given that the human evaluator has rated it **unfavorably**"; in the GPT-4 example (§4.3, Fig. 2): recall in the cases where the human **prefers** GPT-4 = 1852/(108+1852) ≈ **0.945**; recall in the cases where the human **prefers the other** = 118/(160+118) ≈ **0.425**; difference = **0.520** (value of Fig. 1b). The practical reading is: *the judge is right 94.5% of the time when the human sides with GPT-4, but only 42.5% when the human sides with the competitor*.
- **Variant without a human label (§6, Eq. 4; Demographic Parity):** `Bias = P(Y'=1|S=1) − P(Y'=1|S=0)` (difference between the rate at which the judge picks its own response and the rate at which it picks the other's; it does **not** control for quality, which is why the authors warn that it does not prove unfairness).

**2.2 Experiment (§4.2)**
- Data: **Chatbot Arena** (Zheng et al.), **33,000 dialogues** with 1 human question + 2 responses from different LLMs, human label `model_a`/`model_b`/`tie`.
- **8 judges**, all models present in the Arena: GPT-4, GPT-3.5-Turbo, Vicuna-7b, Vicuna-13b, oasst-pythia-12b, dolly-v2-12b, Koala-13b, stablelm-tuned-alpha-7b.
- **Prompt from Zheng et al.** (output `[[A]]`/`[[B]]`/`[[C]]` after an explanation). The judge is **not told** which response is its own.
- **Continuous score (Eq. 3):** `score_A = p(A|context) / Σ_{w∈{A,B}} p(w|context)`, using the probability distribution of the token that follows "[[" (normalizes across models with different distributions; follows Schick et al. 2021). **Position mitigation:** swap A/B and **average** the two scores. Responses outside the format were **excluded**.

**2.3 Perplexity hypothesis (§5)**
- Hypothesis: the judge prefers **more familiar** texts, i.e. those with **lower perplexity** (perplexity = how "surprising" the text is to a model; lower = more probable/familiar), and a model's own texts tend to have lower perplexity.
- Procedure: compute the perplexity of each response **conditioned on the prompt**; take the **perplexity difference A−B**; split into **bins**; in each bin, measure the rate at which each judge declares A the winner **and** the human rate (Fig. 3). Then separate the cases "pair contains the judge's own response" × "pair does not contain it" (Fig. 4), compare the mean log-perplexity of own vs. others' responses (Fig. 5) and repeat with Llama-2/3/3.1 (Fig. 6).
- **Model used to compute the perplexity:** the text does not detail it explicitly; the Fig. 5 caption speaks of "log-perplexity of responses for each LLM evaluator" and the Fig. 3 caption of "LLMs vs human conditioned on perplexity", which suggests the perplexity under the judge itself — **[Interpretation]**, not confirmed in the text.
- **GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-Turbo were excluded** from this analysis "as perplexity values could not be obtained for these models" (§5).

## 3. Main contributions

1. **Quantitative metric** of self-preference derived from Equal Opportunity, comparable to human labels (Def. 4.1).
2. **Measurement on 8 LLMs** on Chatbot Arena: **GPT-4 exhibits significant bias**.
3. **Hypothesis and evidence that the cause is perplexity** (familiarity), and not authorship itself.
4. Proposal (untested) of a **judge ensemble** and of perplexity weighting.

## 4. Key results

- **GPT-4 has the highest bias by Def. 4.1: 0.520** (§4.3, Fig. 1b; reconstruction from the confusion matrix in Fig. 2: 0.945 − 0.425). Followed by **Vicuna-13b and Koala-13b** ("also exhibited significant bias"); the others "displayed values relatively close to zero", and **oasst-pythia-12b, dolly-v2-12b and stablelm-tuned-alpha-7b** negative (reverse bias) (§4.3). Individual values: only in the image.
- **By Demographic Parity (Tab. 2, p. 9):** GPT-4 **0.749**; Vicuna-13b 0.382; GPT-3.5-turbo 0.191; Koala-13b 0.175; Vicuna-7b 0.052; oasst-pythia-12b 0.006; stablelm −0.032; dolly-v2-12b −0.069. The order GPT-4 > Vicuna-13b "aligns closely with the results obtained using Definition 4.1".
- **Qualitative example (Tab. 1):** for "Please list the blue items", GPT-4 opens with "As a language model AI, I can't see the physical objects…"; humans and GPT-3.5 preferred GPT-3.5's direct response, and GPT-4 chose its own. The authors: the bias is **not about factual error**, but about the model's **style/policy** (§4.3, §6) — "advanced models like GPT-4, which thoroughly understand and adhere to their predefined policies, may use the degree of alignment with these policies as a deciding factor when evaluating responses of comparable quality".
- **Perplexity (Fig. 3, §5):** "All models except stablelm-tuned-alpha-7b demonstrated a clear tendency to assign higher evaluations to responses with lower perplexity" (emphasis on **lower perplexity** is mine); the tendency is **stronger than that of humans** in vicuna-13b, vicuna-7b, koala-13b and oasst-pythia-12b.
- **Author vs. others (Fig. 4):** the judges' win rates for own and others' text, in the same perplexity bin, were **similar** (except dolly-v2-12b and stablelm) → "the factor influencing the LLM evaluators' judgments is not whether the response is their own but rather the perplexity of the responses" (§5). Fig. 5: mean log-perplexity is lower for the evaluator's own responses in **all** models.
- **Llama-2/3/3.1 (Fig. 6, §5):** "all models changed their evaluations more than humans, depending on the difference in perplexity" (no human label for these models' responses in the Arena, only comparison with the existing humans).
- **Suggested mitigation (§6):** ensemble of several models; reduce the judge's weight on samples where it has low perplexity. The proposed metric would serve to evaluate these mitigations. **Not tested.**

## 5. Limitations

**From the authors (explicit or implicit):** no perplexity for GPT-4/GPT-3.5 (precisely the most biased/competitive ones), which forced testing the hypothesis on other models (§5); Demographic Parity scores "do not take intrinsic quality into account" and does not prove unfairness (Tab. 2); out-of-format cases were excluded.

**That I identify [Interpretation]:**
- **Correlation, not causation:** the paper shows an association between score and perplexity and that it is independent of authorship *within bins*, but does not intervene (e.g., rewriting the text to change the perplexity and watching the verdict change). The conclusion "the essence of the bias lies in perplexity" (Abstract; §7) is stronger than the evidence.
- **The explanation does not cover the strongest case:** GPT-4 (highest bias) is not in the perplexity analysis, and the authors themselves attribute GPT-4's bias to **style/policy** (§6), not to perplexity. This weakens "perplexity explains the bias" for state-of-the-art models.
- **Human labels from the Arena** come from anonymous voters, with noise; ties and format errors are discarded (sample selection).
- **Pairwise only:** nothing about absolute scoring of a single response (our case), nor about judges with a reference answer (reference-based).
- **2023 models**, several of them weak (oasst-pythia, dolly, stablelm, which barely follow the format). The "8 models" mix good and bad judges; the negative biases of weak models may be just noise.
- **Same family:** the paper does **not explicitly test "same family"** (e.g., GPT-4 judging GPT-4-turbo). What it shows is bias toward the *judge's own* output and toward *familiar* text (low perplexity) in general.
- Metric sensitive to the **balance** of the pairs (recalls conditioned on human preference) and with no confidence interval.
- Eq. 2 and the prose are not perfectly consistent (see §2.1).

## 6. Reflections anchored in OUR project

**R1 — `JUDGE_MODEL` ≠ `GENERATION_MODEL` (P11; `config.py`; `ckpt-0.6-plan.md` §4: all 6 judges with `gpt-4o-mini` = generator).** Position: **(i) strongly SUPPORTS.** The paper shows that self-preference bias is **real and large in GPT-4 (0.520)**, appears **even without the judge knowing the authorship**, and is mediated by **familiarity (perplexity)**. In our design, the generator **is** the judge: `gpt-4o-mini`'s responses are, by construction, of very low perplexity *for `gpt-4o-mini`*. **Question that the paper answers only in part:** *does the bias persist when judge and generator are the same model/family?* — **the paper does not test the (same model) pair under a controlled condition nor "same family"**; it shows that the effect exists for own responses (Fig. 5) and that perplexity, not authorship, moves the verdict (Fig. 4). **[Interpretation]** → *family* matters only insofar as sibling models share data/alignment and therefore familiar style; a judge from **another family** is the safest choice; merely "another size of the same family" reduces but does **not eliminate** the risk. **What the design requires:** (a) `JUDGE_MODEL` from a **different family** than the generator (or, at a minimum, a sensitivity run with 2 judges from distinct families — already in the roadmap); (b) record `judge_model` and `generation_model` in the metadata; (c) report deltas between experiment arms **with the same judge** (the bias is approximately constant across arms of the same generator, so it affects the *absolute level* more than the *delta*; **[Interpretation]**, not tested by the paper).

**R2 — Which judges are most exposed? (`specificity_judge`, `completeness_judge`, `answer_relevance` vs `faithfulness_judge`, `citation_accuracy`).** Position: **(iii) NEUTRAL, with extrapolation of my own.** The paper says that GPT-4's bias comes from **style/policy** and not from factual error. **[Interpretation]:** the **subjective** judges (specificity, relevance, completeness) are more exposed than the **verification** ones against the context (faithfulness, citation), which have an observable basis. This is my hypothesis: the paper only measures general pairwise preference. **We would adopt** prioritizing the other-family judge **first** on the subjective judges.

**R3 — How to measure the bias in our setup (step 0.8; "100 human labels").** Position: **(i) SUPPORTS the need to measure; (ii) CHALLENGES immediate feasibility.** Def. 4.1 requires, for each pair, a **human label of the preference** and **knowledge of which response is the judge's**. The "100 human labels" of the golden set (100/100 reviewed) are, as far as I understand, **review of the reference answer/question**, not judgments of generated responses — **to confirm**. If so, Def. 4.1 **cannot** be computed; alternatives: (a) label ~30–50 pairs of responses (generator A vs generator B) for one judge from each family (cheap: our 100 examples); (b) use the **version without a human label (Eq. 4)** only as a *diagnostic* (judge's mean score for its own family vs another), keeping the authors' caveat that it **does not control for quality**. We would **not adopt** Eq. 4 as an acceptance criterion (it conflates bias and real quality).

**R4 — Perplexity as a diagnostic (P11).** Position: **(iii) NEUTRAL / do not adopt.** Prompt perplexity is not available for OpenAI models (the paper itself could not obtain it for GPT-4/3.5, §5); **[Interpretation]:** the same holds for `gpt-4o-mini` through the chat API (only *logprobs* of generated tokens). We would **not adopt** the perplexity-bin analysis; we **would adopt** only the operational conclusion (diversify the judge).

**R5 — Ensemble mitigation (P11, sensitivity run).** Position: **(i) SUPPORTS, at low cost.** The suggestion in §6 (several judges) matches the "sensitivity run with 2 judges" already planned; with 100 examples × 6 judges × 2 models, the cost is small. **Note:** the paper **did not test** the ensemble; see the reading note `qa-eval-judge-vs-f1.md` (Ho et al.) for evidence to the contrary in the extractive regime.

**R6 — Position and token probability (single-answer judges).** Position: **(iii) NEUTRAL.** The normalization `p(A)/(p(A)+p(B))` and the average over position swaps (Eq. 3) apply to pairwise; our judges are single-answer. **[Interpretation]:** using the *logprobs* of the verdict token (continuous score) could reduce the variance of the boolean judges across runs — an **optional idea**, not mentioned by the paper for single-answer.

**R7 — Cross-reference with the literature.** Zheng et al. (own reading note) is **inconclusive** on self-enhancement; **Ho et al. (2504.11972)** report an **absence** of self-preference in short extractive QA. These results **challenge** generalizing Wataoka to our case, but the regime is different (short, verifiable reference answer vs. open conversation). **[Interpretation]:** the risk is greater where the judgment is about **style** (specificity, answer_relevance) than where it is about **correctness against a reference answer**.

**Summary — what we would adopt:** judge from another family (or 2 judges in the sensitivity run); `judge_model` metadata; labeling a set of **generated responses** to measure the bias; prioritizing subjective judges. **What we would NOT adopt:** perplexity analysis (infeasible on the API); Eq. 4 as an acceptance criterion; the causal conclusion "perplexity = cause" as fact; literal transfer of the GPT-4/Vicuna numbers (2023, pairwise) to single-answer `gpt-4o-mini`.
